# Calibration Anchors: the 7-Dimension Copy Score

**Load this before scoring any copy** (the Phase 4 gate, or any "score this" request). Scores given without anchors drift toward flattery; these pairs define what the numbers mean.

All copy below is written about **Inkpot**, a fictional invoicing app for freelance illustrators. Every product detail and number is invented for demonstration. The craft contrast is the point, not the facts.

How to use the anchors: a 3 reads like the 3/10 example: not broken, just inert. An 8 reads like the 8/10 example: shippable, with a visible reason it works. Most competent first drafts land 5-7. Reserve 9-10 for copy you'd put in a swipe file; if you're scoring everything 8+, you're grading the effort, not the words.

**The gate (recap):** any dimension below 7, or total below 56/70 (48/60 when Proof is n/a because no proof exists yet) → rewrite only the weak dimensions → rescore. Max 3 iterations, then present the best version with the full score history.

---

## 1. Clarity

*Does a distracted reader get the core message in one pass?*

**3/10:**

> Inkpot is a holistic invoicing ecosystem that empowers creative professionals to streamline their billing workflows through intelligent automation and seamless integrations, transforming the way independent creators manage their financial operations.

Why it's a 3: thirty-one words, zero picture. "Ecosystem," "empowers," "streamline" could describe a CRM, a bank, or a meditation app. The reader finishes the sentence and could not tell a friend what the product does. Nothing is wrong grammatically. That's exactly the trap.

**8/10:**

> Inkpot sends your invoices, chases the late ones, and tells you who actually owes you money. You draw. It collects.

Why it's an 8: one read, three concrete verbs, and the reader could now explain the product to someone else, which is the real clarity test. The kicker compresses the division of labor into four words. What would make it a 10: anchoring the stakes too: "...so you stop doing your books at midnight on the 30th."

## 2. Specificity

*Real numbers, named details, concrete mechanisms?*

**3/10:**

> Thousands of freelancers trust Inkpot to save them countless hours every month. Our powerful features dramatically reduce the time you spend on invoicing, so you can focus on what really matters.

Why it's a 3: "thousands," "countless," "dramatically," "what really matters": four unverifiable abstractions in two sentences. Every clause could appear unedited on a competitor's page. There is no detail here that only a real product could produce.

**8/10:**

> Inkpot users send their first invoice 4 minutes after signup. That's the median, not the demo. Late payments drop because every invoice ships with a card link and an automatic nudge on day 7 and day 14. Freelancers who turn nudges on get paid 11 days sooner.

Why it's an 8: numbers with mechanisms attached. "Day 7 and day 14" is the kind of detail only a shipped product has. It does more credibility work than the statistics do. "The median, not the demo" pre-empts the skeptic. What would make it a 10: a visible source on the 11-day claim, so it survives verification (see the AI-reader layer).

---

## 3. Voice

*One identifiable human, or a committee?*

**3/10:**

> At Inkpot, we believe creators deserve better tools. That's why we've crafted a best-in-class invoicing experience designed with your unique needs in mind. Join our community of passionate creatives today!

Why it's a 3: "we believe," "best-in-class," "designed with your unique needs in mind": committee voice with an exclamation point doing the work personality should. No one alive talks like this. Swap the logo and it's any brand's About page.

**8/10:**

> I built Inkpot after my third client "lost" an invoice. Not lost-lost. Lost the way your deposit gets lost. So now the invoice chases them instead of me, and I haven't typed "just bumping this!" since March.

Why it's an 8: first person, a grudge, a coinage ("lost-lost"), and a detail so specific it has to be true ("just bumping this!"). You can hear exactly one human being. What would make it a 10: a voice so distinct you'd recognize the author with the byline removed: that usually takes a real person's actual story, which is why Phase 2 says get the founder's story, don't invent it.

---

## 4. Desire

*Is the transformation rendered as a felt moment, not an adjective?*

**3/10:**

> Inkpot includes automated payment reminders, customizable invoice templates, multi-currency support, and detailed reporting dashboards. Manage all your invoicing needs in one convenient place.

Why it's a 3: a spec sheet. The reader is told what the product *has* and never shown what their life looks like *after*. "Convenient" is the writer asking the reader to do the imagining. That's the copywriter's job.

**8/10:**

> Friday, 4:50pm. Every invoice for the month is already out. Two are already paid. The client who always pays in week six just got his second nudge, and you didn't type a word of it. You close the laptop. That's the product.

Why it's an 8: a scene, time-stamped, that the reader can stand inside. The benefit is a moment ("you close the laptop"), not an adjective. The deadbeat client is rendered specifically enough to be *their* deadbeat client. What would make it a 10: stacking the felt cost of the current state right before it, so the gap between Friday-now and Friday-after physically aches.

---

## 5. Proof

*Is every claim covered by evidence that exists?*

**3/10:**

> Freelancers love Inkpot! "Great app, highly recommend." (Sarah) Our award-winning platform is the trusted choice for creative professionals everywhere.

Why it's a 3: the testimonial proves only that someone named Sarah exists. "Award-winning" and "trusted choice" are claims about claims. No award named, no trust measured. This pattern is worse than no proof, because it signals you looked for evidence and this was the best you found.

**8/10:**

> "I invoiced nine clients in the time my spreadsheet took for two. Two paid the same day: that has never happened in six years of freelancing." Maya R., brand illustrator, Inkpot user since March. She's not the outlier: across 1,400 active freelancers, the median invoice gets paid in 9 days, against the 23-day industry average (source linked below the chart).

Why it's an 8: the testimonial is a mini case study (before state, action, specific outcome, timeframe, and an emotional tell, "never happened in six years") attributed to a named, dated, occupied human. Then the aggregate number generalizes it, with a comparison baseline and a cited source. What would make it a 10: proof density: evidence adjacent to *every* major claim on the page, not one strong cluster.

---

## 6. Urgency

*Is there an honest reason to act now?*

**3/10:**

> Don't wait! Sign up NOW before it's too late! Spots are filling fast and this incredible offer won't last forever!!!

Why it's a 3: panic with no mechanism. Filling fast, why? Won't last, why not? Manufactured scarcity doesn't just fail on its own; readers price it as a lie and discount every other claim on the page. Three exclamation points is the writer audibly not believing it either.

**8/10:**

> Founding pricing, $9/month, locked for as long as you stay, ends at 2,000 accounts. We're at 1,742. After that it's $19, because support costs scale with users and early members took the bigger risk. No countdown timers; the number just runs out.

Why it's an 8: a deadline with a stated reason, and the reason is the persuasion. It makes the scarcity auditable. "No countdown timers" weaponizes the reader's fatigue with fake urgency. What would make it a 10: adding the cost of waiting in the reader's own terms (one more month of 23-day payments on their revenue = a number).

**Scoring note:** Urgency is the one dimension with an honest ceiling. If no real reason to act now exists, a quiet cost-of-inaction line scores 6-7: score it there and move on. Never coach copy toward invented scarcity to clear the gate; that trades the Urgency score against Proof and Voice, and against the reader.

---

## 7. Flow

*Does each line pull to the next, headline to CTA?*

**3/10:**

> Inkpot is an invoicing application. It was founded in 2024. Invoicing is important for freelancers. Many freelancers struggle with cash flow. Cash flow problems cause stress. Inkpot has many features. The features are designed to help. You should try Inkpot.

Why it's a 3: eight flat declaratives of nearly identical length, no connective tissue, no loop, no question, no turn. Each sentence is a full stop in both senses. Reading it feels like climbing stairs; nothing pulls.

**8/10:**

> Here's the math nobody does. Say you bill $6,000 a month and your average invoice pays in 23 days. That's roughly $4,500 of *your* money sitting in other people's accounts. Every day, all year. Inkpot can't make your clients richer. It does something better: it makes paying you the path of least resistance. How? Three ways, and the third one is the reason illustrators switch.

Why it's an 8: short opener ("Here's the math nobody does") with zero friction; the math builds and lands on a number that stings; then a turn ("can't... does something better") that resets attention; then an open loop sized exactly one section ahead. Bucket brigade, rhythm alternation, and a seed of curiosity. All invisible unless you're looking. What would make it a 10: sustaining this for the entire page with no visible seams, and closing that third-way loop with a payoff worth the tease.

---

## After scoring

Present the scorecard plainly: dimension, score, one-line reason quoting or pointing at the specific weak passage (a score with no pointer isn't actionable). Then the priority fixes, at most three, each naming the dimension, the move (from Phase 2 of the skill), and the expected direction. Rewrite only what's weak. If a dimension is stuck after three iterations, the constraint is usually upstream of the copy: thin proof inventory, no real urgency mechanism, or an offer that hasn't earned an angle. Say that instead of polishing harder.
