# Writing rules and the tells that make a long piece sound written by AI

Load at Phase 4 and again at the gate. The rules first, then the tells, each with an invented line that has it and a better one.

## The rules

- **No em dashes or en dashes.** Commas and periods. The scan counts them.
- **Sources as plain names in parentheses.** Links to sources pull readers out and read as a citation dump.
- **Credentials mid-article.** One line, inside the point it proves. "I've run reminders for 300 clinics" belongs in the reason about reminder timing, not in the opening.
- **No income or results promises.** Money math says "arithmetic, not a promise". A hypothetical worked example says it's a hypothetical.
- **Specific numbers, and who counted them.** "2,412" with a source beats "thousands".
- **The honest sections are required.** "What breaks", and the line admitting some numbers are vendor data.
- **Real lists.** Let the editor number them.
- **Prompts that work.** Run each one once on the model it's written for. A prompt that breaks the article's own advice gets caught by the first reader who tries it.
- **One link per paragraph, CTAs on their own line, every link tagged.**
- **No client names** unless the author says so in writing.

## The tells

Read the draft aloud before the gate. Rewrite any sentence that sounds like a slogan.

| Tell | Has it | Better |
|---|---|---|
| A slogan where a sentence belongs: balanced pairs, "X is your new Y", stacked fragments | "Ads get the click. The page gets the call." | "The ad did its job. The page it opened had no phone number." |
| "Isn't X. It's Y." by reflex | "This isn't a marketing problem. It's a follow-up problem." | "Most of the missed revenue came after the lead arrived." |
| A colon that sets up a reveal | "Here's the kicker: nobody reads the reminder." | "Nobody reads a reminder sent at 6am." (A colon before a real list or a code block is fine.) |
| A list of three by reflex | "Faster, cheaper, better." | Two examples, or four, chosen because they're true |
| A staged opening | "Everyone's wrong about no-shows." | Start with the finding: "Most no-shows booked more than two weeks out." |
| A sentence every reader already believes | "Customers want fast service." | Put the surprising finding there, with its source |
| An abstraction as the payoff | "In the end, it's all about trust." | End on the last specific: the number, the step, the picture |
| A closer that sums up | "And that's the whole playbook." | Stop after the last useful line |
| Homework for the reader | "So ask yourself: what's your no-show rate? Are you tracking it?" | Tell them how to find it |
| Hype words | game-changer, unlock, leverage, delve, supercharge | The plain verb |
| Fine print in the body | A paragraph of caveats after every claim | One honest line where it's part of the story; the rest goes in "what breaks" |
| A hypothetical where a real example exists | "Imagine a dentist who..." | A real, public example the reader can go look at |

## The opening, specifically

The first two lines carry the point. No throat-clearing ("In today's fast-paced world"), no question the reader didn't ask, no "In this article, I will". Point, then what it covers and the read time.
