# E-E-A-T Content Examples

How content shows Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trust on the page. Every author and passage below is **fictional and written for this library** to show a pattern. None of them is a real quote, a real business or a real result, and the numbers are illustrative. Do not copy the wording or the numbers. Copy the pattern, then fill it with your own real experience.

---

## What makes content "human"

Before the examples, what separates great human content from AI slop:

1. **A specific person with a specific viewpoint.** Not a committee, not "balanced coverage".
2. **Real experience showing through.** Stories, mistakes, specific numbers from actual work.
3. **Opinions defended with reasoning.** Not hedged, not "some say X, others say Y".
4. **Unique insight or data.** Something you can't find by aggregating other sources.
5. **A voice that could only be this person.** Recognizable even without a byline.

## Example 1: A local service owner explains a failure (fictional)

**Author:** Marisol Vega, owner of a made-up HVAC company in Tampa. **Format:** how-to article, "Why your AC runs for ten minutes and quits".

**Why it works:** she answers the question in the first line, then shows she has seen the problem in real homes.

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** her own service calls, named parts, a mistake she made early on
- **Expertise:** says which causes are common and which are rare
- **Authority:** license number and years in business sit in the byline
- **Trust:** says when to stop reading and call someone

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "Short cycling almost always means one of three things: a dirty filter, a failing capacitor, or a system that is too big for the house. On my own calls the capacitor is the cause more often than the other two combined. It is a forty-minute fix. I once replaced a whole compressor on a unit that only needed one, and I still think about it. If your unit is under ten years old, check the filter and call us about the capacitor before you let anyone quote a replacement."

**What to steal:**
- The answer first, the story second
- A named, specific part instead of "a component"
- One honest admission of her own mistake
- A line that sends the reader away from the expensive answer

## Example 2: A professional states the limits (fictional)

**Author:** Dr. Anika Rao, a made-up dentist. **Format:** cost guide, "What a dental implant costs, line by line".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** describes what she sees at each consult
- **Expertise:** separates the implant, the abutment and the crown, which most pages blur together
- **Trust:** says who is not a good candidate and why

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "The number on a flyer is usually the implant post alone. The abutment and the crown are billed separately, and so is the scan. I will not quote you a total without seeing a scan, because bone density changes the plan. If you smoke or have uncontrolled diabetes, I will ask you to fix that first. That is not me turning down your money. It is me not wanting to place something that fails in two years."

**What to steal:**
- Break a price into the parts the reader will actually be billed for
- Say what you will not promise
- Give the reason behind a "no" so it reads as care, not sales

## Example 3: The sample business shows its pricing (fictional)

**Author:** Dana, owner of Hartline Plumbing, Dallas TX (a sample business). **Format:** page, "Water heater replacement cost in Dallas".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** counts jobs by type, not by total
- **Expertise:** explains tank versus tankless in terms of the reader's house
- **Trust:** shows a real price range with what moves it up or down

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "Most of our tank swaps land between the low and high ends of the range below. What pushes a job to the high end is almost never the heater. It is a gas line that needs to be moved, or a permit the city asks for, or a vent that is no longer up to code. We tell you which of those apply before we start, in writing. If a quote does not name them, ask why."

**What to steal:**
- A real range with the reasons it moves
- The things that surprise customers, named in advance
- "In writing" as a concrete proof point

## Example 4: A contractor admits what insurance will not cover (fictional)

**Author:** Luis Ferreira, a made-up roofer. **Format:** explainer, "Storm damage claims: what to expect".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** has sat through adjuster visits
- **Expertise:** knows the difference between wear and storm damage
- **Trust:** says plainly what a roofer cannot do for you

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "I cannot tell you your claim will be approved, and any roofer who does is selling you something. What I can do is photograph the damage the way an adjuster looks for it, and be on the roof when they visit. About half the claims I help with get approved in full. Some get partial approval. A few are denied because the damage is old wear. I will tell you which one I think yours is before you pay for anything."

**What to steal:**
- Refusing the promise a competitor makes
- A rough, honest outcome split instead of "we get results"
- A commitment that costs nothing to keep

## Example 5: A shop explains the order of operations (fictional)

**Author:** the service manager at a made-up auto repair shop. **Format:** article, "Why we test before we replace parts".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** describes the bench test, not the brochure
- **Expertise:** explains what a code does and does not tell you
- **Trust:** names the one case where guessing is the cheaper path

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "A check-engine code is a clue, not a diagnosis. The code says a sensor reading looked wrong. It does not say the sensor is bad. We have had cars arrive with a new sensor already installed and the light still on. We test the wiring and the vacuum side first, because that is where the money gets wasted. The one time we skip testing is when a part is under twenty dollars and the test takes longer than the swap."

**What to steal:**
- A short rule of thumb the reader can repeat
- An exception that proves the rule is thought through
- Describing the process, not just the result

## Example 6: A practitioner measures instead of assuming (fictional)

**Author:** Priya Nandakumar, a made-up performance engineer. **Format:** analysis post, "How slow is a typical local business site on a phone?"

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** ran the test herself and says how
- **Expertise:** picks a measurement that can be repeated
- **Trust:** lists the limits of the sample

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "I loaded forty contractor homepages on a mid-range phone over a throttled connection and wrote down when the phone number first appeared. The median was later than I expected, and the three slowest pages all had a video behind the headline. This is a small, hand-picked sample, so read it as a pattern worth checking on your own site, not a census. The method and the raw list are linked below so you can rerun it."

**What to steal:**
- State the method in one sentence
- Admit the sample's weakness before the reader does
- Link the data so it can be checked

## Example 7: A newsletter writer uses primary research (fictional)

**Author:** Ben Okafor, a made-up writer covering small-business operations. **Format:** newsletter issue, "How owners actually price emergency calls".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** talked to the people doing the work
- **Expertise:** groups answers into patterns
- **Trust:** reports the answers that surprised him

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "I asked thirty owners one question: how do you decide what to charge for a 2am call? I expected most to say they follow a price book. Only a handful did. The rest start with what it costs to roll a truck at that hour and add what they think the caller will tolerate. One owner told me, 'I'd rather lose the call than apologize for the price later.' That sentence changed how I read the rest of the answers."

**What to steal:**
- The question you asked, stated exactly
- A named kind of source ("owners") instead of "experts"
- One short real-sounding quote that carries the finding

## Example 8: A tester shows every variant (fictional)

**Author:** Camille Duret, a made-up kitchen tester. **Format:** recipe article, "The best way to sear a steak at home".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** cooked many versions and kept notes
- **Expertise:** explains why, not only what
- **Trust:** reports the variant that failed

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "I cooked the same cut six ways: cold pan, hot pan, flipped every thirty seconds, flipped once, salted early, salted late. Flipping often cooked faster and more evenly, which surprised me, because I was taught the opposite. Salting early gave the best crust. Cold-pan searing was the one I would not do again. The photos of all six are below, including the bad one."

**What to steal:**
- Name every variable you changed
- Report the result that contradicted what you were taught
- Show the failure, not only the winner

## Example 9: An investigator shows how she verified it (fictional)

**Author:** Noor Haddad, a made-up security reporter. **Format:** report, "A vendor's breach notice does not add up".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** original reporting, not aggregation
- **Expertise:** knows what to check and in what order
- **Trust:** separates what she confirmed from what she was told

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "A source sent me a sample of the data. Before writing anything I checked whether the records were real: I matched a dozen entries against public listings and asked two people on the list to confirm theirs. Ten matched. The company's statement says no customer data was exposed. The sample suggests otherwise, and I have asked the company to explain the difference. I will update this post when they answer."

**What to steal:**
- Describe the verification steps
- Say what is confirmed and what is not
- Promise an update, then make the update

## Example 10: A writer teaches with two contrasting stories (fictional)

**Author:** Elliot Marsh, a made-up personal finance writer. **Format:** essay, "Two ways a plumber retires".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** describes situations he has seen and labels the two cases as composites
- **Expertise:** reduces a complicated topic to one idea
- **Trust:** labels composites as composites

**Illustrative passage (invented, a labeled composite):**

> "Take two plumbers who each earn the same year after year. One buys a bigger truck every time business is good. The other puts a fixed slice of each job into an account he does not look at. Ten years later the first has a nicer truck and the second has a business he can sell or step away from. Neither is wiser. One of them just decided once and stopped negotiating with himself."

**What to steal:**
- Two contrasting cases, one idea
- A plain-language conclusion with no jargon
- An honest label when a story is a composite

## Example 11: A practitioner calls out industry boilerplate (fictional)

**Author:** Tessa Lindgren, a made-up recruiter. **Format:** article, "Why every job post sounds the same".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** reads job posts for a living
- **Expertise:** knows what candidates skip
- **Trust:** admits her own old posts were bad

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "'Fast-paced environment' and 'competitive pay' tell a candidate nothing. I wrote posts like that for years and they performed like it. The ones that worked said what a Tuesday looks like, what the first month is for, and what the pay actually is. If you are hiring technicians, say whether they will be on call. People will apply for a hard truth faster than a vague perk."

**What to steal:**
- Name the exact clichés
- Admit your own past mistakes
- Replace the vague line with the specific one

## Example 12: An engineering team shows the approach that failed (fictional)

**Author:** the platform team at a made-up software company. **Format:** engineering post, "What we got wrong about scheduling".

**E-E-A-T signals:**
- **Experience:** describes a real system they ran
- **Expertise:** explains the trade-off, not only the fix
- **Trust:** shows the failed first attempt

**Illustrative passage (invented):**

> "Our first design assigned each customer to a single worker. It was simple and it worked until one customer's batch job starved everyone sharing that worker. We spent two months trying to tune around it before accepting that the design was the problem. Here is what we shipped instead, and the one thing it makes harder."

**What to steal:**
- The failed attempt, told plainly
- The cost of the final choice, not only its benefits
- Time spent, stated as a fact

## The pattern across all examples

### 1. First-person experience
Not "studies show" but "I found" or "we saw". The writer is present in the work.

### 2. Specific details
Not "many" or "significant" but a named part, a method, a count you can check.

### 3. Named kinds of sources
"Owners I asked", "two people on the list", "the city's permit office". Not "experts say".

### 4. Positions you can disagree with
A claim with a reason, not clickbait contrarianism.

### 5. Admitted limits
"This will not work for everyone", "I am not sure why", "I was wrong about this".

### 6. A clear voice
You could guess the author without a byline.

### 7. Original insight
Something you could not get by aggregating other pages.

## How to apply these examples

When writing SEO content, ask:

1. **What is my specific experience with this topic?** (If none, maybe do not write it.)
2. **What numbers can I cite from actual work?** (Not made up, not "research suggests".)
3. **What is my actual opinion here?** (Not both sides, your side.)
4. **What would I tell a smart friend who asked about this?** (That is your voice.)
5. **What can only I say about this?** (That is your unique value.)

If you cannot answer these questions, you are probably writing AI-style content even if AI did not write it. Never invent a quote, a client or a number to fill a gap. Use a placeholder and ask the user.

---

## Red flags to avoid

Even human writers fall into these traps:

- **Aggregation without insight.** "Here are 10 things I read about X" with no point of view.
- **Research without application.** Citations but no "here is what I did with this".
- **Balance without conviction.** "Some say X, others say Y" and never committing.
- **Expertise without stories.** Technical depth but no human moments.
- **Generic advice.** Could apply to anyone, helps no one specifically.

---

## The test

Before publishing, compare your draft to the patterns above:

- Does it answer the question first, like Example 1?
- Does it break a price into what the reader will be billed, like Examples 2 and 3?
- Does it refuse a promise a competitor makes, like Example 4?
- Does it state its method and its limits, like Examples 6 and 9?
- Does it show the failure, like Examples 8 and 12?

If your content has none of these qualities, it will feel AI-generated even if it is not.

## Writers worth reading for craft

These are pointers, not quotes. Read the originals and notice the pattern yourself.

- **Paul Graham's essays:** plain statements of a view, argued one step at a time.
- **Wait But Why:** long explanations built on analogies, with the research visible.
- **Stratechery:** one named framework, applied again and again.
- **Matt Levine's Money Stuff:** complicated mechanics explained with everyday comparisons and dry humor.
- **Serious Eats, The Food Lab:** tested variants with the results reported.
- **Julia Evans:** starts from what confused her and says what she is still unsure about.
- **Dan Luu:** measures instead of assuming.
- **KrebsOnSecurity:** original reporting with the verification steps shown.
- **Derek Sivers:** short posts, one position each.

Use these as benchmarks. If your content would not hold up next to a page that shows its method, its limits and its author, it is not ready.
