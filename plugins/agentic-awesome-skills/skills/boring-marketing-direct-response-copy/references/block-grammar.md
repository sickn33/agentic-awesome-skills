# Block Grammar: What Each Section of a Page Owes the Reader

Load when writing a full landing page, sales page or service page, when writing an offer
stack, guarantee, price section or FAQ, or when reviewing a finished page. It comes from
studying live direct-response pages across education and coaching, software, e-commerce,
agencies, classic direct response and local services. `/boring-marketing-landing-page` decides which
sections go on the page; this file decides what goes in them.

Every strong page does the same jobs in roughly the same order, and each job has a copy
grammar the best pages follow. Learn the grammar and you stop asking "what do I say here"
and start asking "what does this section owe the reader". All examples below are
illustrative.

## Job 1. The promise

### The headline

One sentence. The reader's future, not the product's features. It survives being read
alone, out of context, with no image. Length: about 6 to 14 words; past 20 you're writing
a paragraph in headline clothes.

The five patterns that recur:

1. **Outcome plus timeframe.** "Get paid in 9 days, not 40." Strongest when the timeframe
   is specific.
2. **The named enemy.** "Your plumber isn't expensive. Surprise bills are." Sets up the
   whole page.
3. **The mechanism as the promise.** "One camera. One video. One price." Works when the
   mechanism is genuinely different and named.
4. **Permission.** "You can start lifting at 50." For readers who need permission before
   instruction.
5. **The specific number.** "How 1,400 freelancers get paid without chasing anyone." The
   number does credibility work before the proof section arrives.

What fails: category names as headlines ("The complete invoicing platform"), two
promises joined by "and", and anything where the reader's situation is absent. If the
headline would fit a competitor's page unchanged, it isn't a headline.

### The subheadline

One sentence that answers "how" or names the audience. If the headline is the outcome,
the sub is the mechanism; if the headline is the mechanism, the sub is the outcome. Never
the headline again in longer words.

### The eyebrow

Three to eight words that name the audience or the format so the wrong reader can leave.
"For Shopify sellers doing $1M to $10M." "Free workshop, 45 minutes." "East Dallas
plumbers since 2009." Never a slogan.

### Hero patterns by business type

- **Education and coaching:** promise, the founder's face or a video, a count (students,
  members, clients), price or trial terms visible. The count in the hero is near
  universal.
- **Software:** promise, the product visible in a frame, an email field or trial button, a
  rating badge, and the friction line ("No card required") under the button.
- **E-commerce:** one offer or one hero product, a promo bar with a code or shipping
  threshold above, a trust strip (guarantee, shipping, warranty) right under.
- **Agencies and advisory:** one promise, one ask, and the ask is a free deliverable (a
  plan, an audit, a call), rarely a price. Founder or team photos on the direct-response
  ones.
- **Classic direct response:** a pre-head ("ATTENTION: for runners over 40 who..."), the
  headline, a video, then a "prefer to read? scroll down" bridge.
- **Local services:** the phone number is the largest element in the header. The hero is a
  single field (zip) or a short intake form. The promise is same-day, upfront pricing or a
  named guarantee. See `local-patterns.md`.

## Job 2. Proof under the hero

The most consistent block on strong pages and the most skipped in first drafts. Something
proves the promise within one screen of it.

- **The label matters more than the logos.** "Used by 12,000 freelancers" beats an
  unlabeled logo row. Give the count.
- **Operating metrics over vanity metrics.** Retention, median result, days to first
  result, jobs completed.
- **Rating with the count.** "4.9 from 1,284 reviews", never "5 stars" alone.
- **Credentials as badges, with dates.** "Third-party tested, March 2026", "Licensed
  master plumber, TX #____".

What fails: grey logos with no label, "as seen in" when the mention was a directory
listing, a rating with no count.

## Job 3. The problem

### The manifesto

Two to six short paragraphs, 120 to 250 words. Write the reader's situation in their
vocabulary, then name the real problem, which is never the obvious one. The recurring
shape:

1. What everyone in their position has already tried, without judgment. ("Every seller we
   talk to has tried a bookkeeper.")
2. What they got instead. ("A P&L that says profit and a bank balance that says otherwise.")
3. Two or three questions they can't answer. ("Where did the money go? Which products
   actually make money?")
4. The reframe, two lines, the second turning the first. ("The problem isn't your
   bookkeeper. It's books that ignore your inventory.")
5. One line naming the category you're creating. ("This is e-commerce accounting.")

What fails: the problem in your words instead of theirs, blaming the reader, and a reframe
that only restates.

### Before and after columns

Pain bullets against gain bullets, parallel in number, length and grammar. The pain side
specific and slightly uncomfortable, the gain side concrete. "Before: a four-hour window
and a price by phone. After: a text with the tech's photo and the price before he rings."
Readers score themselves down the left column.

### Qualification gates

"Is this for me" and "who it isn't for" lists give permission to leave, which raises the
credibility of everything else. Write the "not for you" side honestly and include one item
that costs you customers. "You want to do it yourself. Get the free guide instead."

## Job 4. The mechanism

Name it. A named method is remembered and repeated; an unnamed list isn't. Three steps,
three words if you can: Import / Send / Collect. Inspect / Price / Fix. Each step gets a
title, one sentence of what happens, and one of what the reader gets. Answer "what
actually happens", not the theory.

The founder's own before-and-after is often the first case study, used as proof of the
mechanism before any client result. If the founder has a story with numbers, it goes here.
For software the mechanism is the product: each row is a job it does, in the customer's
words ("Chase late invoices for you"), not the feature's ("automated dunning").

## Job 5. Credibility

### Case cards

The headline of a case card is two numbers and an arrow. "38 days → 9 days to get paid."
"6 → 41 booked jobs a month." "10K in 58:10 → 51:40." Then the unit and period, one line
of starting context, the name and role. A quote is optional, one sentence, in their voice.
What fails: percentages without the base ("300% growth"), no starting number, a card
headlined with the client's name.

### Review summaries

If you write the paragraph above a review widget, say what people actually say, including
the mild criticism. "Customers most often mention the price matching the quote and the
text before arrival. The few critical reviews are about weekend availability." One honest
sentence does more than ten five-star quotes. Never invent topic percentages.

### Testimonials

Lead with the result in the first clause, then the praise. Attribution needs a role and a
place; "Sarah M." alone isn't a person. One to three sentences; anything longer is a case
card or a video.

## Job 6. The offer, itemized

### The offer stack

Every item gets a name, a one-line description of what it does for them, and a value. The
value must be defensible: a price you actually charge, or the cost of the nearest
alternative.

```
The 12-Week Plan        3 runs + 2 strength sessions a week     $297
Weekly video check-in   12 form reviews from a coach            $360
The Injury Playbook     what to do when something hurts         $49
Bonus: Race-week guide  taper, fueling and pacing, week 10      $29
                                               Total value     $735
                                         What you pay today    $197
```

(Illustrative.) The last two lines are the device: a total that's large and true, then
the actual ask, made small by contrast. "What you pay today" names the moment.

### Bonuses

Each bonus has a number, a name that sounds like a product, one line of what it does, and
a value. A bonus should remove a specific objection. One that removes "I won't have time"
is worth three that add content.

### Pricing cards

A "Best for" line under every plan name does the qualifying. One badge only ("Most
popular" or "Best value", never both). Annual as the default with the saving in money,
not percent. An "All plans include" line under the cards catches the reassurance that
would otherwise clutter each card. For physical products, per-unit math is the game ("$1.55
a serving").

## Job 7. Reduce uncertainty

### The guarantee

Specific, measurable, a sentence you'd sign: the number, the window, what happens if you
miss. "Do the sessions for 6 weeks. If your easy pace hasn't improved, full refund." "The
price on your quote is the price on your invoice." Then one line on what is and isn't
counted, then the terms without weasel words. "Satisfaction guaranteed" guarantees nothing.

### The price section

- Pay in full beside the payment plan, both visible.
- A strikethrough needs a reason next to it ("Founding price, ends at 500 members") or it
  reads as fake.
- The time cost: "About three hours a week for twelve weeks." It removes the biggest
  unspoken objection for programs.
- The guarantee restated at the price. Doubt peaks at the number.
- The price story: why the price is what it is (what it costs to deliver, what it
  replaces). A price with no story invites haggling.
- A deadline, if one exists, with a date, a time and a time zone. A deadline without a
  date is noise; an invented one is a lie.

### Comparison

Two different blocks. **Versus alternatives** (us, an agency, a hire, doing it yourself)
reframes the price; its first column is the decision ("Cost to start", "Time to first
result", "Cancel anytime"), and rivals' cells are written honestly. **Plan matrix** helps a
decided buyer pick a tier. Don't merge them.

### The FAQ

Questions in the reader's own words, including the rude ones. "Is this really free?" "Do
I need to know how to code?" "Why is your price higher than the guy on Craigslist?"
Answers in three to five sentences: the direct answer, the reason, the edge case. Never
use the FAQ to sneak in features. The last question is often the ladder step: "What if I
want it done for me?"

## Job 8. One action

- **Button copy:** first person or outcome-specific. "Send me the plan" beats "Submit".
  "Get my price" beats "Learn more".
- **Under every button,** the one line that removes the live objection: the count, the
  friction ("No card. Cancel in one click."), the guarantee, a dated deadline, or the time
  ("Two minutes. We reply within one business day.").
- **Repetition:** the same CTA, same words, down a long page. Changing the wording makes
  it read as two offers.
- **Application pages:** when the price isn't on the page, the form carries it. Ask plain
  questions (budget band, timing). The booked-confirmation page says what happens on the
  call and gives one thing to do before it.

## Review a finished page

Every "no" is a rewrite. Would the headline survive alone? Is there proof within one
screen of it? Is the problem in the reader's words, with a reframe? Is the method named,
in three steps or fewer? Does every case have a starting and ending number? Does every
stacked item have a defensible value, and every bonus a named objection? Is the guarantee
signable, with a number and a window? One action, one label, a line under every button?
Is there one sentence a competitor couldn't have written? Read it aloud: does it sound
like a person who figured something out, or like a company?
