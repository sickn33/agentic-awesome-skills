# The structure: twelve parts, why each works

Load at Phase 3. Each part has a job, the reason it works, and a deletion test: if the article still holds without the part, cut it. The examples come from three invented articles, all made up for this file:

- **(SaaS)** A scheduling-app founder writes "How Small Clinics Cut No-Shows Without Hiring Anyone", a how-to for clinic managers.
- **(Agency)** A freelance video editor writes "How to Start a Podcast Clip Agency", an opportunity playbook for creators and freelancers.
- **(Local)** Hartline Plumbing, Dallas TX (a sample business), writes "The Dallas Homeowner's Guide to Replacing a Water Heater", a guide for its customers.

Numbers in the examples are placeholders or labeled illustrative. A real article fills them from `sources.md`.

## Three shapes, one spine

| Part | Playbook (an opportunity, for practitioners) | How-to (for users or buyers) | Customer guide (a business to its customers) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Why this market | Why sell to these businesses | Why this problem costs you now | Signs it's time, for your house or car or teeth |
| What to offer | The menu of offers | The options, and when each fits | Your choices, and which fits whom |
| The money math | What they pay against what it costs you | What the problem costs against what fixing it costs | Planned against emergency, or option against option |
| How to do it | Find, pitch, deliver | Set it up, step by step | What to ask, what to check, how to book |

Eras and the scorecard are optional in a customer guide. Everything else stays.

## The twelve parts

### 1. The point and the read time
**Job:** in two sentences the reader decides it's for them; one more line says what it covers and how long it takes.
**Why it works:** a clear claim plus an honest time cost earns the scroll. Naming the length signals the piece is complete, so the reader saves it instead of skimming.
**Example (Agency):** "The easiest agency to start this year sells to podcasters who are behind on clips. I'll cover who to sell to, what to charge and how to do the work in an afternoon a week. It's long, about an 18 minute read."
**Deletion test:** never cut.

### 2. The size of the gap
**Job:** two or three named numbers on how big the problem is, then the contrast that creates the gap: who should be solving it and isn't.
**Why it works:** readers believe a gap they can measure. The contrast is what makes it an opportunity rather than a fact.
**Example (SaaS):** "[A named study's share of clinic appointments missed each year], while [a named survey's share of small clinics that still remind patients by phone call, during business hours]."
**Deletion test:** cut only for a short how-to where the reader already feels the problem.

### 3. The whole idea in four steps
**Job:** the whole article as a short numbered list, right after the gap.
**Why it works:** a skimmer gets everything from it, and every later part maps to one step, so the reader always knows where they are.
**Example (Local):** 1. Check the age on the label. 2. Price the swap before it leaks. 3. Choose tank or tankless on your water use, not on the ad. 4. Book it for a weekday.
**Deletion test:** never cut.

### 4. How we got here
**Job:** four or five eras, each a bold label and two sentences, then the one trend under all of them.
**Why it works:** history turns an opinion into a direction. When the reader sees the trend, "now" feels inevitable instead of hyped.
**Example (Agency):** the show-notes era, the audiogram era, the clip era, the one-click clip tool era, and the editor-with-taste era now starting. The trend: making clips got cheap, choosing the right ninety seconds didn't.
**Deletion test:** cut from a customer guide unless the history changes the decision (a rule change that altered what's sold, say).

### 5. Why this audience, or why now
**Job:** five or six numbered reasons, each a bold claim, a named number and a plain consequence. Then a scorecard the reader can use, and one real story.
**Why it works:** reasons with consequences let readers test the claim against their own world. The scorecard turns your opinion into their tool, which is what gets saved.
**Example (Agency):** "**Podcasters already pay for editing.** [A named survey's share of podcasters who pay an editor.] Clips are the next thing on the same invoice." Scorecard: rate a show 1 to 5 on episodes a month, guest quality, current clip output, and whether they sell something.
**Deletion test:** in a how-to, shrink to three reasons; never cut the consequence.

### 6. What to offer
**Job:** sell the result, not the tool. Then the menu: four to six options, each with who it's for, how it works, and a real public example to study.
**Why it works:** a reader can't act on a principle. A menu with real examples they can go look at is something they can copy today.
**Example (Local):** tank, tankless and heat pump, each with "fits a house that..." and the trade-off in plain words.
**One allowed device:** a single "not X, but Y" line that renames the offer in the buyer's words ("not a scheduling tool, a full Tuesday"). Used more than once, it reads as a slogan.
**Deletion test:** never cut from a playbook.

### 7. The money math, "arithmetic, not a promise"
**Job:** what the market pays (sourced) against what it costs to deliver now (itemized); price in layers; one worked example with round numbers, labeled; two rules; an admission that the gap narrows over time.
**Why it works:** itemized costs are believable where a single big number isn't. The label protects the reader and the author, and admitting the gap narrows is the line that makes the rest trustworthy.
**Example (SaaS, illustrative):** "Say a clinic books 40 visits a day and 1 in 10 don't show, at $120 a visit. That's $480 a day of empty chair. If reminders win back a third of those, it's about $160 a day. That's arithmetic, not a promise. Use your own numbers."
**Deletion test:** cut from a customer guide with no money decision in it.

### 8. How to do it
**Job:** the setup (a folder, a tool list or a checklist), then numbered steps, each with a copyable prompt or an exact action, then one first-hand result with real counts.
**Why it works:** a first-hand count ("I tried this on 12 shows; 9 had no clips under a minute") is the most believable line in the piece, because the author did the work.
**Example (Agency):**
```text
You're editing [show name], episode [n]. From the transcript, find the
three moments under 60 seconds where a guest says something specific and
surprising. For each: the start and end timestamps, the first line as a
caption, and one sentence on why someone would share it. Skip anything
that needs the earlier conversation to make sense.
```
**Deletion test:** never cut.

### 9. Keeping it working
**Job:** the few numbers to check every week, and the routine around them.
**Why it works:** a reader who knows what to measure keeps doing the thing. A client who sees those numbers on Monday stays.
**Example (SaaS):** no-show rate, same-day rebookings, and reminder replies, every Monday, in that order.
**Deletion test:** cut from a customer guide; keep in playbooks and how-tos.

### 10. What breaks
**Job:** six or seven honest failure modes, each bold-led, including "believing the headline numbers" (the vendor data you quoted).
**Why it works:** readers who've tried something know where it fails. Naming the failures first makes everything else believable.
**Example (Local):** "**Buying on the sticker.** The cheapest unit often needs a new vent or a bigger gas line, and that's where the quote doubles."
**Deletion test:** never cut.

### 11. Start this week
**Job:** a Monday-to-Friday list, or the first five actions with times.
**Why it works:** it converts reading into doing. It is also the part people screenshot.
**Example (SaaS):** Monday, pull last month's no-shows. Tuesday, write the three texts. Wednesday, turn them on for one provider...
**Deletion test:** never cut.

### 12. The close
**Job:** one concrete last line in the author's voice, a picture rather than a moral. Then, if the author sells something, a divider and one short paragraph with one link per line.
**Why it works:** a moral tells the reader how to feel and reads as filler. A picture they can see ends the piece on the author's voice.
**Example (Local):** "Look at the label on yours tonight. If it says 2014, you have a weekday to choose, or a Saturday night to find out."
**Deletion test:** never cut.

## A sequel to an earlier piece

When the author already published a guide on the same field, the sequel gets a narrower angle, not a rerun: one worked example walked end to end, one role per part, or one hard step in depth. A one-line pointer back to the earlier piece is enough. Copying its gap stats, eras or start-this-week list makes readers feel they've read it.
