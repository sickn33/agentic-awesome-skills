# Archetype Patterns

Load when structuring an edition (SKILL.md Phase 1). These are **structural** patterns:
what job each section does, how the curation/original ratio works, how the edition paces.
They deliberately contain no voice. No openers, no greetings, no sign-offs, no emoji
conventions. Voice comes from `./brand/voice-profile.md`; if you find yourself copying a
phrase from this file into a draft, you've misused it.

Each pattern names the reader's job-to-be-done, the section anatomy (jobs, not text), the
curation:original ratio, pacing, and the failure mode that kills that archetype.

---

## 1. Roundup

**Reader's job:** "Catch me up on what mattered in this niche since the last edition, with
judgment I trust."

**Anatomy (section jobs, in order):**
1. *Orientation*: one or two sentences naming the week's through-line, if one exists. Earns
   the scroll; not a table of contents.
2. *The lead item*: the single most consequential development, given 3-5x the space of any
   other item: what happened, why it matters to this audience, the writer's read.
3. *The body items*: 3-6 developments, each with what happened (2-3 sentences) + one judgment
   line. The judgment line is the product; the summary is commodity.
4. *Quick hits*: one-liners for things worth knowing but not discussing. Optional.
5. *Close*: the single CTA.

**Ratio:** ~50/50 curated facts to original judgment. Every item carries a take.
**Pacing:** descending importance. Front-load; readers leak with every section.
**Length band:** 800-1,500 words. Weekly cadence fits best.
**Failure mode:** flat item lists where every story gets equal weight and no opinion:
indistinguishable from a feed. If you can't rank the items, you haven't formed a view yet.

## 2. Deep-dive

**Reader's job:** "Teach me one thing thoroughly enough that I can act on it this week."

**Anatomy:**
1. *The question*: state the exact question the edition answers, early and concretely. The
   strongest deep-dives are question-shaped: a real reader question or a situation the
   audience is in right now.
2. *Stakes*: one short passage on why the answer matters and who it's for. Permission to
   skip is a gift to the wrong reader and a hook for the right one.
3. *The answer structure*: the framework, process, or argument in 3-5 named components,
   each with explanation + one concrete example. Components need memorable names only if the
   names do work; forced acronyms cost credibility.
4. *Application*: how to use it, as steps, a worked example, or a decision rule. This section
   separates a deep-dive from an essay; without it the reader nods and does nothing.
5. *The artifact*: a template, checklist, or copy-paste resource when the material supports
   one. The artifact is the most-forwarded part of this archetype.
6. *Compression*: the insight in 2-3 sentences, for the reader who skimmed.

**Ratio:** 90%+ original. External sources appear as evidence, not content.
**Pacing:** slow is fine if every section pays; trim examples before trimming structure.
**Length band:** 1,500-3,000 words. Weekly or biweekly; this archetype doesn't survive daily.
**Failure mode:** the survey: covering a topic instead of answering a question. If the title
could end in ": an overview", restart from a sharper question.

*Timeless structural reference, the question→framework→artifact arc:* the pattern behind
the most durable paid newsletters (Lenny's Newsletter made it canonical): open on a verbatim
reader question, build the answer as a named framework with credibility anchored in where it
was learned ("we used this at [company]" beats "experts say"), close with a usable template.
Steal the arc and the credibility-anchoring, not anyone's phrasing.

## 3. Personal-essay

**Reader's job:** "Give me a perspective I can't get elsewhere, from a person I'm getting to
know."

**Anatomy:**
1. *The moment*: a specific scene, observation, or admission that earns the abstraction to
   come. Specificity is the hook: a date, a place, a number, a conversation.
2. *The turn*: where the particular becomes general, the thesis, stated plainly.
3. *The case*: why the writer believes it, with experience, evidence and counterargument honestly
   handled. First-person reasoning is the genre; hedging to neutrality kills it.
4. *The hand-back*: what the reader does with it: a question to sit with, a reframe, a small
   experiment. Reflection questions work when they're sharp and few (1-3, not 5+).
5. *Compression*: the idea in one or two lines.

**Ratio:** 100% original. No link sections: links dilute intimacy, the archetype's asset.
**Pacing:** narrative, not scannable. This is the one archetype where long unbroken paragraphs
can be correct. Scannability rules from the skill apply loosely here.
**Length band:** 800-2,000 words.
**Failure mode:** the journal entry. A moment with no turn. If a stranger can't say why the
story mattered, the essay isn't done. Second failure: borrowed profundity (quotes and
platitudes doing the thesis's job).

## 4. Curated-links

**Reader's job:** "You read everything so I don't have to. Show me only what's worth my
time, and tell me why."

**Anatomy:**
1. *Entry note*: 1-2 sentences of context for the week's batch. Optional; skippable weeks
   should skip it.
2. *The flagship*: the one item worth the whole edition, with the longest commentary (3-5
   sentences: what it is, the writer's take, who should act on it).
3. *Grouped picks*: 4-8 items organized by reader mode (to read / to try / to watch), each:
   link + 1-2 sentences of commentary that adds something the source doesn't say.
4. *Close*: single CTA, often "reply with what I missed" (replies are deliverability gold).

**Ratio:** 30% original by word count, 100% original by value. The selection IS the product.
**Pacing:** fast. Every item must survive the forward test (SKILL.md Phase 3); a 5-link
edition of bangers beats a 10-link edition of "fine".
**Length band:** 500-1,200 words. Weekly or daily.
**Failure mode:** the link dump: items without commentary, or commentary that restates the
headline. The reader can get the links anywhere; they subscribe for the *why this one*.

*Timeless structural reference, curation-with-a-take:* the pattern behind every durable
curation letter (Ben's Bites-school): each link earns its slot with a stated reason ("this
matters because", "I'm skeptical of X but"), and items are grouped by what the reader will do
with them, not by source. The taste is the moat; the commentary is the proof of taste.

## 5. News-briefing

**Reader's job:** "Get me current in five minutes, and tell me what it means, daily or near
it."

**Anatomy:**
1. *Top line*: one sentence on the single biggest thing. The skim-reader's whole edition.
2. *Lead story*: what happened (2-4 sentences, facts straight) + an explicit *why it
   matters* line framed for this audience's decisions, not for the general public.
3. *Second tier*: 2-4 stories at half the lead's depth, same shape: facts, then implication.
4. *Quick hits*: one-liners. Volume lives here so the top stays clean.
5. *Recurring closer*: a number-of-the-day, a poll, a tool pick: one small slot the reader
   learns to expect. Habit is this archetype's entire business model.

**Ratio:** 70% reported facts / 30% implication. The implication line ("why it matters", "the
bottom line") is mandatory per story: facts without consequence are a wire service.
**Pacing:** ruthless. Total read ≤5 minutes. Cut stories before compressing all of them into
mush.
**Length band:** 500-1,000 words. Daily to 3x/week; weekly briefings drift into roundups.
**Failure mode:** straight news. If a story's framing would fit any outlet, re-angle it for
this audience or cut it. Second failure: burying the lead to seem comprehensive.

*Timeless structural reference, selective-bolding + bottom-line discipline:* the
Morning-Brew-school craft rules that survive every redesign: bold only the single most
surprising clause per story (bold everything = bold nothing), and end each story with one
labeled takeaway line the skimmer can read on its own. Structural discipline, not voice.
The humor layer those letters add is *their* voice profile, not the pattern.

## 6. Hybrid

**Reader's job:** "One idea worth thinking about, plus the week's worth-knowing links. My
one subscription if I keep only one."

**Anatomy:**
1. *The original piece*: a compact deep-dive or essay (500-900 words) following a shortened
   version of those anatomies: question/moment → answer/turn → application/hand-back.
2. *The seam*: a visible break. Readers should know they're switching modes; blending the
   essay into the links muddies both.
3. *The link section*: 3-6 curated items per the curated-links anatomy, commentary included.
4. *Close*: one CTA, attached to whichever half matters more this week.

**Ratio:** ~60/40 original to curated, flexing week to week. The structure absorbs a thin
week on either side, which is why solo operators end up here.
**Pacing:** the original piece carries the open; the links reward the finish.
**Length band:** 1,000-1,800 words. Weekly.
**Failure mode:** two mediocre halves. The hybrid is forgiving of imbalance but not of
flatness. Each week, one half must be strong enough to carry the edition alone. If neither
is, the fix is cutting a half, not padding both.

## Cross-pattern rules

- **Consistency is structure, not sentences.** Recurring sections train readers where to
  look: keep them stable for months. Recurring sentence-level moves (same opener shape, same
  transition, same close) are template rot. The anti-template check in SKILL.md Phase 4
  exists to catch exactly this.
- **Front-load without exception.** Every archetype puts its best material above the first
  scroll. "Building to it" is for essays' internal arcs, never for edition order.
- **Pick by job, audit by click.** Choose the archetype from the reader's job; then let the
  list's per-link click data (SKILL.md Phase 2) tell you whether the curated or original
  material is carrying the letter, and shift the ratio, not the archetype, first.
