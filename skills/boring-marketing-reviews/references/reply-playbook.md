# Reply playbook

**Load this when** writing owner replies (Phase 4). Patterns by case, each with a sample
for the sample business Hartline Plumbing, Dallas TX (illustrative). Match the owner's
voice; these show the shape, not the words to reuse. Every reply here is a draft for the
owner to post.

## Who reads a reply

Not mainly the reviewer. The next customer, comparing three plumbers, reads the bad
reviews and the owner's answers to decide how they'd be treated if something went wrong.
Write for that reader. A calm, specific reply to a 1-star review often does more for the
next call than ten thank-yous.

## Timing

Bad reviews: within one business day where possible. Everything else: within a week.
Google says to respond "in a timely manner"; most customers expect a reply within a week
(see the facts file). A reply posted months late still helps the next reader, so clear
the backlog, newest first.

## Praise with details (5 stars, a story)

Pick up one detail, name the person who did the work if the review did, and stop.
Two or three sentences.

> Thanks, Maria. Glad Luis got the water heater swapped before your in-laws arrived
> Saturday. We'll pass this along to him.

## Praise with no text (5 stars, no words)

A short line is enough, or skip it if there are dozens. Don't paste the same line on
every one; vary it or leave some unanswered.

> Thanks for choosing us, Daniel. Call anytime.

## Good with a gripe (4 stars, "great work but...")

Thank them, answer the gripe in one sentence with what you're doing about it. These are
the reviews where a reply changes the reader's mind the most.

> Thanks, Priya. You're right that the 8-to-12 window was too wide. We've started
> texting when the tech is 30 minutes out so you're not stuck waiting at home.

## Mixed (3 stars)

Same as above, with a way to reach the owner if something is still unresolved.

## Genuine complaint (1 or 2 stars)

Shape: acknowledge, own what's true, say what changed or what you'll do, give a name and
a direct way to reach the owner. No arguing the details in public, no blaming the
customer, no job details they didn't share.

> Robert, I'm sorry. Being quoted one price on the phone and another at the door isn't
> how we want to work, and I've changed how we give phone estimates because of it. I'd
> like to make this right. Please call me directly at the office and ask for Sam.

What not to write:
- "We're sorry you feel that way." It reads as not sorry.
- A paragraph proving the customer wrong. Even when you're right, the reader sees a fight.
- The job address, the invoice amount, what was found in the house, or anything else
  the reviewer didn't put in the review.
- Threats, mentions of lawyers, or asking them to take it down. In the US, threats and
  intimidation to remove reviews fall under the FTC review rule.

## Not a customer, or a review that looks fake

Check the records first: name, phone, address, job dates. If there's no match, or it's
clearly about a different business, or it comes from a competitor or an ex-employee,
report it from the Business Profile with the policy reason (not a genuine experience,
conflict of interest, off-topic). Then reply anyway, briefly and without accusing anyone,
because removal can take weeks or never happen.

> We can't find a job under this name and want to understand what happened. Please call
> the office and ask for Sam so we can look into it.

## Reviews that name a tech

Name the tech back in a positive reply. In a negative one, don't name or blame the tech
publicly; the owner owns it.

## Health, dental and med spa businesses

In the US, never confirm or suggest the reviewer is a patient, never mention a treatment,
visit or result, even if they did. Reply in general terms about how the practice works
and invite them to call. Fines for review replies that disclosed patient information
have happened.

> We take every concern seriously and would welcome a call to the office so we can talk
> it through.

## Reviews in another language

Reply in the language of the review when the owner or staff can write it well; otherwise
reply in English with a short thanks.

## Lines that don't help

- Stuffing the service and city into every reply ("Thanks for choosing Hartline, the best
  emergency plumber in Dallas TX"). It reads as spam to the person reading it, and
  nothing shows it helps ranking enough to be worth that.
- The same opener on every reply. Scroll a rival's profile and you'll see why.
- Asking the customer to update their review in the reply. Ask privately after fixing
  the problem, if at all, and never with an offer attached.
- Signing with the owner's personal cell number on every reply. Use the main line or a
  name to ask for.
