# The coverage map: the brief for a committed topic

Load in Phase 4 before writing any brief for the committed family. The coverage map replaces the keyword outline. It tells a writer what to answer, in what shape, with which claims, and where the citation is actually winnable. It optimizes for answer completeness and for being the cited answer, never for keyword density. Northpeak (scheduling software for multi-location teams) is a sample business, and every number is illustrative.

## The bar

A writer can execute it without reconstructing the evidence, and a senior strategist would send it as is. A brief that restates the keyword and says "write a helpful article" fails however tidy it looks.

## What goes in it, in this order

1. **Working title and meta description.** A writer starts from a title, not a topic string.
2. **The angle, as a short story.** Two to four sentences: what you observed, who owns the answer today, why this business can take it.
3. **Where the answer comes from today.** The source kinds the observed answers lean on (roundups, threads, reference pages, product pages) and what that means for the page: a topic answered from roundups wants a comparison-shaped page with a clear verdict, one answered from reference pages wants clean definitions and named entities.
4. **The questions, grouped by the job they do** (the definition seekers, the comparison shoppers, the skeptics, the people mid-problem), each group with one line on why it matters. Never a flat dump of 18 rows.
5. **The answer map**, one row per question:

| Question (verbatim) | Source | Answer shape | Gap and its observed reason | Who answers it today | Section |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

   - **Source:** People Also Ask, AI-native (asked of assistants, from forums, reviews, sales calls), autocomplete, Search Console, or **derived**. Derived means the topic clearly needs it but nobody was observed asking. Label it that way; never dress an inference up as a real question.
   - **Answer shape:** direct (one sentence), definition, list, table, steps, comparison. The shape sets the block an engine can lift.
   - **Gaps first.** A gap is where the citation is winnable, and it names what you observed: **brand absent** (missing from the observed AI answers or AI Overview for this question), **answer incomplete** (the observed answers and their sources don't actually answer it), or **weak source** (the answer leans on a thin forum post or scraped list a solid page could displace). A gap you can't point at isn't a gap, and "competitors cover this better" about a page nobody read isn't one either.
6. **The page as a writer builds it.** Section order; what each section must accomplish; which questions it answers (every question lands in some section); the liftable block it produces (definition box, comparison table, numbered steps, a short FAQ).
7. **Depth benchmark.** What the measured winners actually carry on the surface you're targeting: the cited sources for AI answers, the top organic results for Google. Word count, number of self-contained answers, number of concrete facts. Length follows the benchmark; it's never a habit. One cited winner might carry 2,500 words with eight standalone answers and seventeen numbers while a longer page with one answer goes uncited, which is why completeness and liftable density are the bar, not length.
8. **Entities to name.** Products, standards, people, places, from brand context or observed evidence only.
9. **The claim contract, in three plain lists.**
   - **May say:** self-descriptive facts the business has confirmed ("runs on phones and desktop", "connects to the three payroll tools listed on the integrations page"), with the exact approved wording.
   - **Must not say:** anything nobody can back: invented customer counts, guarantees, speed claims, compliance claims.
   - **Needs checking first:** comparisons, numbers, outcomes, testimonials, certifications and prices whose only source is the business's own marketing copy, plus anything the owner hasn't confirmed. If it's in this list, the draft says nothing about it until someone confirms it.
10. **Voice direction** a writer can apply mechanically ("short sentences, contrast instead of adjectives, let the comparison do the work").
11. **Open items** in plain words: the lived examples or numbers the writer needs from the business, asked for precisely ("two shift-swap stories from customers with five or more locations").
12. **One evidence note** at the foot: the check dates, the engines and location, and what wasn't measured.
13. **One next step.** The single approval or missing input that moves this brief forward. Not a menu.

## The failure modes

- **The keyword outline.** Headings that mirror the keyword, no observed questions, no gaps, "add an FAQ". A writer learns nothing new.
- **The invented question.** A "People Also Ask" row nobody observed.
- **The ungrounded gap.** A gap with no observed reason behind it.
- **The laundered claim.** A comparison or performance claim moved to "may say" because it's on the homepage. Being on the homepage is attribution, not proof. This is the most important failure to catch, and the prettier the brief, the more damage it does.
- **The false-ready handoff.** Calling the brief ready while items sit in "needs checking first" or open inputs are unanswered.
- **The near miss.** Real questions, right shapes, honest claims, and no gaps: every question is already well answered. It's valid and nearly useless. Say no winnable white space was found and ask whether this topic is worth a page at all.
- **The database export.** Field names, status words, ids or internal labels in the writer-facing brief. Keep the machinery out; the evidence note carries the receipts.

## Worked example: Northpeak (sample business, illustrative)

**Working title:** Shift scheduling for multi-location teams: what changes after the third location
**Meta:** How multi-location managers schedule across sites without double-booking, with the rules that differ by state and a comparison of the tools built for it.

**The angle.** In the answers we checked, assistants recommend generic single-site schedulers for multi-location questions and cite roundups that never test cross-location features. Northpeak's own data covers 1,200 multi-location teams (illustrative), so it can answer the question the roundups skip: what breaks when one manager schedules several sites.

**Where the answer comes from today.** Mostly roundups and two forum threads where restaurant managers compare tools. The page needs a comparison table with a verdict per team type, and one original number the roundups can quote.

| Question | Source | Shape | Gap | Who answers today | Section |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "how do I schedule staff across multiple locations?" | AI-native | steps | Brand absent: 0 of 4 observed answers name Northpeak | Two roundups, one forum thread | 2 |
| "can employees swap shifts between locations?" | People Also Ask | direct | Answer incomplete: the cited sources describe single-site swaps only | A help-center page of a competitor | 3 |
| "best scheduling software for multiple locations" | Search Console | comparison | Weak source: the top cited page is a 2023 listicle with no testing | An affiliate roundup | 4 |
| "what is predictive scheduling law?" | People Also Ask | definition | None: well answered by government pages | State labor sites | 5 (short, links out) |
| "how many hours ahead must a schedule be posted?" | derived | table | Not checked | | 5 |

**Claim contract.**
- May say: "Northpeak lets a manager see every location's schedule on one screen" (on the product page, confirmed by the team).
- Must not say: "the most popular scheduler for restaurants" (no source).
- Needs checking first: "cuts scheduling time in half" (homepage copy only), the 1,200 teams figure (needs a dated export from the product team).

**Open items.** Two real cross-location swap stories; the dated team count.

**Evidence note.** Answers checked on ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google, signed out, US, on the date shown in the plan; AI Overview captured for 3 of 5 questions; Gemini not checked.

**Next step.** Confirm the team count, and the brief is ready to write.
