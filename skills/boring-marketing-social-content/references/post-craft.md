# Post craft: the seven rules with examples, and the tells

Load before the first draft of a session and before the first SCORE pass. Every example below is invented for this file. The examples rotate across three kinds of users: a SaaS founder, a creator or agency owner, and a local business.

## The seven rules

### 1. One finished post per request

A founder asks for "a post about the new booking page". The weak delivery is five drafts and a two-week plan, which hands the choosing back to the person who asked. The strong delivery is one post, finished, with one line under it: "Runner-up: a tutorial drop walking through setup. Ask for it if you want it."

Plans, series and variants are for when the user asks for them, as in CADENCE.

### 2. Something to save

Ask "what would someone come back to this post for?" before writing a word.

- **Local.** A roofing company wants to post about storm season. The opinion version: "Storm season is here. Make sure your roof is ready." Nobody saves that. The save version: the four photos to take after a hailstorm before calling anyone, as a short numbered list.
- **SaaS.** A founder changed pricing. The opinion version argues why usage pricing is fair. The save version shows the old and new pricing page side by side and the exact email sent to customers, which other founders will copy.
- **Creator.** A video editor has a take on hooks. The save version is the three opening lines that held viewers past ten seconds on their last twenty videos, with the retention graph.

If the source has no usable part, build one from facts you already have: their process as numbered steps, a real price explained, the question customers ask most. Ask the user only when there is truly nothing. Don't draft the opinion and hope.

### 3. The reader's tool before your receipts

An agency owner wants to post that they tested review-request texts for six clients and the short one won. Lead with the text itself:

```
Send this the day you finish the job:
"Hi [name], thanks for having us out today. If we did right by you,
a Google review helps a small shop more than you'd think: [link]"
```

The six-client test makes its own post next week. A post that opens on "we tested" makes the reader wait for the part they can use.

Anything handed out has to work. Send the text to your own phone. Run the prompt on the model it names. Click through the setting on a real account.

### 4. A plain first line, then what to do

- **Creator.** "YouTube lets you test up to three thumbnails on one video." Line two: "Put your two best rejects in as B and C on your next five uploads and let it pick." A cleverer first line ("Your thumbnail is lying to you.") makes the reader decode it and loses to the plain one.
- **Local.** "Most plumbers' Google profiles list 'plumber' and nothing else under services." Line two: "Add your top ten jobs, worded the way customers search them."

The first line says what happened or what's true. The second says what the reader does about it.

### 5. A real screenshot before a designed image

- **Agency.** A post about a business whose ad promises 24/7 while its page doesn't say it. The real page on a phone, the missing line circled, the business name blurred, beats a designed "ad vs page" graphic. Blur any business that isn't yours.
- **SaaS.** The changelog line or the docs paragraph, highlighted, beats a branded announcement card.

The screenshot is the receipt. It also makes rule 6 easy, because the reader can check you.

### 6. Wording that matches the source

- The help page says posts with links "may get less reach". The post doesn't say "LinkedIn kills posts with links".
- The report says "one of the fastest-growing". The post doesn't say "the fastest-growing".
- The changelog says "beta, rolling out". The post doesn't say "now available to everyone".

Check every verb, superlative and number against the source before delivering.

### 7. Finished means ready to publish today

A marketing agency asks for five launch posts. The weak delivery: three posts, plus two
"templates" full of `[INSERT CLIENT RESULT]` and a note to spend 45 minutes researching
competitors before filling them in. The user can't post those, so they were never posts.

The strong delivery: five posts written from what the brief already holds (the services,
the price, how the work gets done, a question prospects always ask), each publishable as
is. One of them carries a single inline flag, `[verify: the 3-day turnaround is still
true]`, and reads fine if the number changes.

- At most one verify flag per post, and zero is better.
- No blanks, no "insert X here", no research homework before a post can go out.
- No scores, archetype labels or rubric notes in the delivery. One line at the end can
  say what the posts are built from.

## The tells

Each distinct tell costs 5 points in SCORE (capped at 25). Read the post aloud; rewrite whatever sounds like a slogan. The patterns matter more than the exact words.

| Tell | Has it | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Balanced pairs | "Ads get the click. Pages get the call." | "The ad worked. The page it opened had no phone number on it." |
| "X is your new Y" | "Your Google profile is your new homepage." | "More people saw the profile than the site last month: 1,900 against 240." |
| Stacked fragments | "No call. No text. No booking." | "Nobody called back, so they booked the next company." |
| "Isn't X. It's Y." by reflex | "Churn isn't a pricing problem. It's an onboarding problem." | "Most of the accounts that left never connected a calendar in week one." |
| Sentences that all open the same way | "You need... You need... You need..." | Vary them, or make it a real list |
| A closer that sums up or lands a punchline | "And that's why speed wins." | End on the last specific, or stop |
| A staged opening | "Everyone's wrong about cold email." | Start with the finding |
| Announcing a point instead of making it | "This chart explains why your launch flopped." | Say what the chart shows |
| A sentence every reader already believes | "Customers want fast replies." | The surprising finding, with its evidence |
| A colon that sets up a reveal | "The result: 3x more bookings." · "Here's the kicker:" | "Bookings tripled in the first month." A colon before a real list or a pasted block is fine. |
| A list of three by reflex | "Faster, cheaper, better." | Two examples, or four, chosen because they're true |
| Homework for the reader | "What's your reply time? Are you tracking it? Worth checking." | Tell them how to find it |
| An abstraction as the payoff | "It all comes down to trust." | The number, the step, the picture |
| Several quotes from one source | Four fragments of the same article | One quote; the screenshot carries the rest |
| Fine print in the post | Three caveats after the main point | Caveats go in a reply; one honest line is fine if it's part of the story |
| A hypothetical where a real example exists | "Imagine a dentist who..." | A real, public example the reader can look up |
| Em dashes, "Let that sink in", "Here's the thing", hype words (game-changer, unlock, delve, leverage), "Agree?" closers, emoji-led openers, hollow superlatives, fake humility | | Plain sentences |

## A before and after

A SaaS founder's draft, scored 52:

> Here's the thing: onboarding isn't a feature problem. It's a focus problem.
>
> We cut our setup from 9 steps to 3. The result? Activation went way up.
>
> Simplicity wins. Every time.

Tells: "Here's the thing" (-5), "isn't X. It's Y." (-5), a colon-style reveal ("The result?") (-5), a closer that lands a punchline (-5). No save: there's nothing to copy.

Rewritten, scored 78 (the numbers stand in for the founder's real ones from the credential bank):

> We cut our setup from 9 steps to 3. Here's where each of the 6 went:
>
> 1. Company size: asked later, inside billing
> 2. Logo upload: defaulted to initials
> 3. Invite teammates: moved to day 3, after the first booking
> 4. Time zone: read from the browser
> 5. Calendar color: removed
> 6. Welcome video: replaced by a sample booking on the first screen
>
> Week-one activation went from 31% to 47%.
>
> If your setup has more than 3 steps, list each one and write where it could go instead.

The save is the list, and it comes before the receipt (the activation number). The first line is plain, and the post ends on something the reader can do.
