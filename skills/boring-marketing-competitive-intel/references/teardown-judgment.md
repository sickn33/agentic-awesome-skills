# Teardown judgment

Load before Phase 3 synthesis in any mode. Evidence packs give you what competitors say and charge; this file is how to read what they *mean*. None of this is perishable platform fact. It's interpretation discipline.

## How to read a pricing page

Packaging is the most honest document a competitor publishes, because it's the one place they can't posture. It has to map to how they actually make money.

- **Tier names reveal the ICP ladder.** "Starter / Pro / Enterprise" says they want to land small and expand. "Team / Business" with no individual tier says solo users are a support cost they've decided to fire. The tier they visually highlight is the customer they're built around. Everything else is upsell scaffolding or deflection.
- **What gates each tier reveals what their best customers value.** The feature locked behind the top tier is the one their highest-payers can't live without. That's their real moat as *they* see it. Features given away free are ones they've concluded carry no pricing power (often because the category commoditized them, a useful map of where NOT to differentiate).
- **Margin anxiety shows up as meters.** Usage caps, seat minimums, overage pricing, "fair use" footnotes: each meter marks a cost they can't absorb. A competitor metering API calls has COGS exposure there; you can position against the anxiety itself ("flat pricing, no surprise bills") if your cost structure allows it.
- **Hidden pricing ("Contact sales" only) means one of two things:** deals are genuinely bespoke (real enterprise), or they're price-discriminating and afraid of comparison. Check their reviews for "pricing surprised us" complaints to tell which.
- **Recent discounting, a new cheaper tier, or an extended trial = demand softness.** Confident companies raise prices and shorten trials. Track this in WATCH mode. Pricing moves are the highest-signal delta you'll catch.

## Strategic positioning vs copy accidents

Not every claim on a homepage is a decision. Before mapping a claim as "contested" or "saturated," decide whether it's load-bearing:

- **Load-bearing claims repeat.** The same idea appears in the H1, the ads, the pricing-page header, and the founder's social bio. That's strategy, and they'll defend it.
- **Accidents appear once.** A subhead written by whoever last touched the page, never echoed elsewhere. Don't counter-position against an accident; it can vanish in their next deploy, taking your differentiation with it.
- **Age the claim.** Wayback-check (or note from WATCH history) how long the H1 has stood. A headline stable for a year is identity; one that changed three times in six months means they haven't found product-market message fit, which is itself an exploitable gap (the category's vocabulary is up for grabs).
- **"For everyone" copy is the absence of a decision**, not a broad strategy. A competitor who names no ICP anywhere has left every specific audience unclaimed, which is the cheapest white space there is.

## Why paid claims outrank organic claims

What a company SAYS (homepage) costs nothing. What it PAYS to say (ads) is audited weekly by a conversion number. When the homepage and the ads disagree, **the ads are the truth** about what converts in this market right now.

- A competitor whose homepage says "enterprise-grade platform" but whose ads all run "set up in 5 minutes" has learned that speed sells and prestige doesn't. That's free market research they bought for you.
- **Ad longevity is the strongest single signal.** An ad running 90+ days is paying for itself; an account churning fresh creative weekly is still searching. Weight one six-month-old ad over ten launched last week.
- **No ads at all** means: bootstrapped/sales-led (check headcount and "talk to sales" weight), or paused (cash or CAC trouble; cross-check the struggling signals below). Either way the paid channel is uncontested for you.
- Hooks that appear in EVERY advertiser's rotation are over-bought: real demand, brutal auction. The play is the adjacent claim that reframes it, not the same claim said louder.

## Struggling vs scaling: the tells

Read these as a cluster, never alone. Two or more together is a verdict.

**Struggling:** changelog/blog silent 6+ months; discounting or a suddenly cheaper tier; ads stopped after sustained spend; homepage chasing the current hype noun unrelated to their core; careers page empty or gone; support/community channels with unanswered threads; review velocity falling while old complaints go unanswered.

**Scaling:** shipping cadence accelerating; prices *raised* (the confident move); ad spend broadening to new platforms/geos; new named ICP or vertical pages appearing; integration directory growing; hiring in sales and support (capacity for growth, not just building).

Why it matters for the gap analysis: never build your quarter around outflanking a struggling competitor. They may exit the field on their own. Position against the scaler's structural weaknesses (the things growth makes worse: complexity, price, support queues), because those gaps widen as they win.

## Reading lead magnets and conversion offers

A competitor's lead-magnet shelf tells you where they think their buyer is stuck, and how desperate they are for pipeline.

- **The format reveals the funnel stage they're starving in.** Top-of-funnel guides and templates mean they need awareness; ROI calculators and comparison sheets mean they're losing late-stage deals; "book a demo" as the only offer means marketing has been subordinated to sales.
- **Gating depth is a confidence read.** Email-only gates mean they trust nurture; 6-field forms with phone numbers mean an SDR team is quota-hungry, and their reviews will usually show "got sales calls after downloading" complaints you can position against.
- **A free tool (calculator, grader, audit) is their most expensive offer**: someone built and maintains it. It marks the value prop they consider strongest. An interactive tool nobody in the category has built is one of the cheapest differentiation wedges available.
- **Stale magnets** (dated stats, last year's title) on a current homepage mean the growth team moved on or shrank; cross-check the struggling signals.

## Reading momentum (changelog, blog, launches)

- **Cadence beats content.** Weekly small ships signal a healthy team; a quarterly burst followed by silence signals release-train bureaucracy or layoffs. Compare the last 90 days to the 90 before it: acceleration or deceleration is the finding, not the absolute count.
- **What the launches cluster around is next quarter's positioning.** Three consecutive releases touching the same area (say, permissions and audit logs) means an enterprise push is coming. Expect their pricing and homepage to follow. You're reading their roadmap in public.
- **Launch theater vs substance:** a "launch" that's a webinar, a rebrand, or a template pack is marketing filling a shipping vacuum. Count only releases that change what the product does.

## Reading AI-citation presence

- Being cited in "best <category>" answers and roundups is the new default-awareness layer: buyers increasingly arrive with a shortlist an AI wrote. A competitor cited everywhere has presence you must out-position, not out-rank.
- **Absence is the opportunity flag.** A well-funded competitor invisible to AI surfaces has a moat-free flank; a category where citations all trace to 2-3 stale listicles is one fresh comparison page away from capture (hand that to /boring-marketing-seo-content and /boring-marketing-ai-search).
- Distinguish *roundup citations* (live, checkable, fixable) from *model-knowledge presence* (lags reality by the model's cutoff). A young competitor will be under-represented in model knowledge regardless of traction. Don't read that as weakness; check the live roundups instead.

## Reviews as your roadmap: weighting the complaints

G2/Capterra/Reddit complaints about a competitor are a pre-validated backlog: each one is a buyer who paid, hit a wall, and articulated the gap in their own words.

- **Weight by recurrence × recency × tier.** One angry review is noise. The same complaint across 5+ reviews in the last year, from the competitor's core tier, is a structural gap they likely *can't* fix (it's usually downstream of an architecture or pricing decision).
- **Sort complaints into "won't fix" vs "haven't fixed."** "Too expensive for small teams," "too complex," "support is slow since they grew" are strategy-level: fixing them would break their model. Those are yours. "Missing X integration" is a sprint away, so don't build positioning on it.
- **Praise is as useful as complaints:** what reviewers love about them is saturated territory (they own it); what reviewers love *despite* ("great product, awful onboarding") hands you the wedge message verbatim.
- Pull the complaint language verbatim into the evidence. It's pre-made VoC and the exact phrasing `/boring-marketing-positioning-angles` and `/boring-marketing-direct-response-copy` should echo. Flag it for `/boring-marketing-audience-research` to mine deeper.

## Synthesis discipline

- Three evidenced gaps beat ten observations. If a "gap" has no evidence-pack line behind it, it's a hypothesis. Label it or cut it.
- A gap is only exploitable if competitors *can't cheaply close it*. "Their pricing page is ugly" is a weekend fix; "their packaging fires the segment you serve" is structural. Recommend only structural gaps.
- White space has two causes: nobody claimed it because it's hard to deliver (opportunity), or because nobody wants it (trap). Before recommending white space, find one piece of demand evidence (a review complaint, a Reddit thread, an unbought ad angle) that someone is asking for it.
