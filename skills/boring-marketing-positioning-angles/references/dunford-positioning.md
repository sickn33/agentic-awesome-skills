# Positioning Before Angles (Dunford)

Load in Phase 1 when the problem is bigger than messaging: the business is in the wrong
category, chasing the wrong customers, or losing deals on price it should win on value.
From April Dunford's "Obviously Awesome". Positioning is the context a buyer uses to judge
the offer; the same product looks expensive in one frame and cheap in another.

## The five components

**1. Competitive alternatives.** What customers would do if this didn't exist: a
spreadsheet, an agency, a freelancer, a YouTube playlist, the big franchise, doing
nothing. Rank them by how often customers actually mention them (ask sales, support or the
office manager what buyers tried first).

**2. Unique attributes.** What this business has that the alternatives don't: a feature,
a method, a team background, equipment, a data set, hours, a niche nobody else serves.
List without judging yet.

**3. Value.** What each attribute lets the customer do. Run the "so what" chain until you
reach time, money or worry:
- Inventory-aware reconciliation, so COGS is right, so the P&L matches the bank, so the
  seller stops making buying decisions on fake profit.
- Stocked trucks, so the heater is replaced on the first visit, so there's no second day
  off work.

**4. Best-fit customers.** Who cares most about that value. Find them in the history:
which customers had the best margin, the least support load, the best results and
reviews? That's the segment, described narrowly ("Shopify sellers doing $1-10M with
physical inventory", "owners of pre-1970 houses in East Dallas").

**5. Market category.** The frame that makes the value obvious. The same company can be "a
bookkeeper" (compared on price with everyone) or "inventory accounting for e-commerce"
(compared with three firms); "a plumber" or "trenchless sewer repair". The category also
decides which searches the business appears for, so it ties to page titles and, for a
local business, the Google Business Profile primary category.

## The statement

For [best-fit customers] who [situation], [product] is the [category] that [key value].
Unlike [primary alternative], we [the attribute that proves it].

- "For Shopify sellers doing $1-10M who carry inventory, [the firm] is the e-commerce
  bookkeeping service that reconciles cost of goods to stock every month. Unlike
  generalist bookkeepers, we close books that match your bank account." (an illustrative firm)
- "For East Dallas homeowners with older cast-iron drains, Hartline Plumbing (a sample
  business) is the sewer and drain specialist that shows you the camera video before
  quoting. Unlike plumbers who quote from the driveway, we price the pipe you actually
  have."

## Signs it's a positioning problem, not an angle problem

- They win when buyers come to them first and lose when buyers compare.
- Their best customers are one kind, and the site talks to everyone.
- They're priced above the market and reviews praise the quality, yet the site competes
  on "affordable".
- The category they're filed under (by search, by marketplaces, by assistants) isn't
  where their value shows.

## Mistakes

1. Positioning for everyone. For a local business, drive time and crew capacity also say
   no to the 40-mile radius.
2. Leading with credentials or features. Translate them to value.
3. Copying the leader's position. You can't out-big them in their own frame.
4. Ignoring non-competitor alternatives (spreadsheets, DIY, doing nothing).
5. Picking a category nobody searches for. Narrow it, but keep it a phrase buyers use.
