# VoC Mining Judgment: Signal vs. Noise

Load this BEFORE mining (every Phase 2 subagent) and again before Phase 3 synthesis.
This is judgment, not perishable fact. No TTL.

## Where the signal lives in reviews

**Ignore star ratings as a sorting mechanism; read 2-4 star reviews first.**
- 5-star reviews are thin: short praise, incentivized submissions, honeymoon-period
  enthusiasm. Occasionally useful for desire language ("finally I can..."). Skim, don't dwell.
- 1-star reviews are rage plus edge cases: shipping disasters, billing disputes, user error.
  Useful for objection language and dealbreakers, but the anger drowns the reasoning.
- 2-4 star reviews contain *trade-off reasoning*: "I love X but Y" sentences where the
  customer weighs what matters. This is where buying criteria live in their own words.

**Weight specificity over sentiment.** "Great product, highly recommend" carries zero
information. "I switched after the third time the export silently dropped rows and I sent a
client a broken report" carries a pain, a switching trigger, a stakes statement, and
vocabulary, all in one sentence. A specific lukewarm review outweighs ten glowing
generic ones. Specificity is also your astroturf filter (below).

**"I wish it..." sentences are product-positioning gold.** "I wish it...", "if only it...",
"the only thing missing is...", "it would be perfect if..." are unmet desires stated in
buying language by people who already paid. Tag these `desire`, intensity-bump them +1,
and quote them whole. They tell positioning what the *next* purchase decision hinges on.

**Competitor 1-star reviews reveal switching triggers.** The moment someone publicly quits
a competitor is the moment marketing should meet them, and the review names the moment
exactly ("after the second price hike in a year I was done"). Tag these `switching-trigger`.
Your own product's 5-star reviews sometimes contain the mirror image: "I came from X
because...", equally valuable, same tag.

## Astroturf and pollution detection

Discount or flag (don't silently include) when you see:
- Clusters of reviews in the same week with similar phrasing, usually a campaign.
- Marketing vocabulary in a "customer" mouth: "game-changer," "seamless," feature names
  spelled exactly as the homepage spells them, benefit statements in brand voice.
- Generic praise + perfect structure + no usage detail. Real customers ramble and
  misspell; copywriters don't.
- Reviewer profiles with one review ever, or a string of 5-stars across one company's
  product line.
- Disclosed incentives ("received product for review"), not fake, but sentiment is bought;
  keep only the specific complaints from these.
- On Amazon: check the variant. Reviews pool across colors/sizes/generations, and a quote
  may describe a product you're not researching.

## Platform-specific reads

**Reddit.** Comments beat posts: the post asks, the thread answers. Sort by top-of-year
for durable pains, by new for current language. The OP's question itself reveals awareness
level (how they describe the problem before anyone names a product). Watch for vendor
employees in comments (new accounts, one-product enthusiasm). Threads titled "best X for
Y?" and "alternatives to X?" are the densest pages on the internet for this work.

**YouTube comments.** Mine comments on *category and competitor* videos, not the brand's
own (already-converted audience). Question comments ("does this work if I...?") are
objections in the wild. Comments with timestamps point at the claim that mattered most to
viewers. Comparison-video comments are pre-purchase deliberation verbatim.

**Forums/communities.** The recurring thread ("monthly vent thread", "what are you using
for X") beats the pinned FAQ. Long-tenured members' complaints carry more weight than
drive-by posts. They've exhausted the workarounds.

**X/LinkedIn.** Identity and hot takes, not deliberation. Good for dominant-emotion reads
and vocabulary; weak for buying criteria. Quote-tweets and replies under a viral category
complaint are richer than the complaint itself.

## Quote hygiene

- Verbatim means verbatim: keep typos, slang, profanity, ALL CAPS. The texture *is* the data.
- Ellipses for trims only. Never merge two quotes into one. Never "clean up" grammar.
- Record the date. Customer language drifts: a 2023 complaint about a since-fixed bug is
  history, not VoC. Undated quotes get "undated", and synthesis weights them lower.
- One quote, one URL. A quote you can't source is a quote you don't have.

## Intensity rubric (1-5)

- **1**: mild preference, politeness-register ("would be nice if")
- **2**: stated annoyance, no stakes ("kind of annoying that...")
- **3**: concrete cost named: time, money, rework ("wasted half a day re-doing...")
- **4**: emotional language, identity or reputation stakes ("made me look like an idiot
  in front of my boss")
- **5**: action taken or vowed: churned, switched, demanded refund, warns others
  ("cancelled on the spot and told my whole team to avoid")

Frequency tells you what the category is; intensity tells you what the campaign is.
Lead-pain candidates need at least one 4-5 quote. A pile of 2s is table stakes no matter
how tall.

## Two traps in synthesis

**The negation trap.** When customers repeatedly say what they *don't* want ("not another
dashboard," "without the enterprise sales call"), those words are poisoned for the
category. Record them in Vocabulary as words copy must avoid, and note the desire hiding
underneath the negation.

**The vocabulary gap.** List the words customers use next to the words the industry uses
for the same thing ("keeps everything in one place" vs. "centralized data platform").
The gap is the copy opportunity. Downstream skills should write the customer's column.
